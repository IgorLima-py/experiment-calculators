The advanced version: spending the error deliberately

A constant corrected threshold (Pocock) is the easiest to act on, but it is strict at the final look, which is where most of the information is. O'Brien-Fleming spends almost nothing early and nearly the full budget at the end — the usual choice when you want the option to stop early without paying much for it.

LookPocock zPocock pO'Brien-Fleming zO'Brien-Fleming p

Both are computed live by the same recursion that produced the headline number, at your significance level.

What this assumes, and what it does not cover
  • Equally spaced looks. The looks are assumed to divide the data evenly. Peeking mostly early is less costly than this says; peeking mostly late is worse.
  • You would have stopped at the first significant result. That is the standard model of peeking. If you saw significance and kept running anyway, this arithmetic does not describe what you did.
  • A two-sided z-test on accruing, independent data, with the normal approximation — the same footing as the sample size calculator.
  • This is retrospective. It tells you what looking has already cost. It cannot rescue a test you have already stopped: a result that only cleared 0.05 is not made valid by reading this page.
  • The prospective fix is a different tool. If you want to monitor continuously and stop whenever you like, you need always-valid inference — mixture SPRT or confidence sequences — set up before the test starts. That is what Statsig, Optimizely and GrowthBook run internally. Platforms with batched daily data, like Spotify, tend to choose group-sequential methods instead — which is the family this page belongs to, and why discrete looks are the right model here.
  • Not covered: futility stopping, several metrics at once, unequal information between looks in the headline figure, and Bayesian stopping rules (which are not immune to this problem either).

The recursion behind this page is checked against a multivariate-normal integration in scipy, against the values published by Armitage, McPherson & Rowe (1969), Pocock (1977) and O'Brien & Fleming (1979), and against a million-run Monte Carlo — see the published comparison.
