Experimentation calculators

Calculators are a commodity. Judgement is not.

There are fifty free A/B test calculators. Almost none of them tell you what the number means, and none of them tell you what would make it a lie. These do both.

Every result on this site comes with one plain sentence explaining what it actually means and — the part that matters — what would invalidate it. The arithmetic is the easy half. Knowing that your sample size assumes you never looked at the results early, and that looking five times turns your 5% false-positive rate into 14%, is the half that decides whether the number was worth computing.

Why you should believe any of it

Because you do not have to. A calculator that is subtly wrong is worse than no calculator: it is publicly checkable and permanently embarrassing. So every formula here is cross-checked against a reference implementation — statsmodels, scipy, numpy — and the comparison is published, including the places where implementations legitimately disagree and why.

Three things that check found, all of which would otherwise have shipped silently:

  • The Student t distribution lost every significant digit near zero at large degrees of freedom, because the textbook form of the identity rounds to exactly 1.
  • The detectable-effect formula that cluster-trial guidance publishes does not deliver the power it claims — it is out by up to two thirds of a percentage point, worst exactly where geo tests operate. This site inverts the exact function instead and shows both.
  • A data parser read the digit in "Market 1" as that market's revenue, returning a mean of 13 where the answer was 53,806.

It also explains where widely used tools disagree rather than pretending they do not. Evan Miller's sample size calculator returns about 6% fewer users than this one; the reason is the variance convention under the null hypothesis, it is not a continuity correction and not Cohen's h, and both are defensible. The comparison is in the open.

What this is

Plain HTML and JavaScript. No framework, no build step, no backend, no tracking, nothing to install, no email form in front of anything. Inputs live in the URL, so any result can be sent to a colleague as a link. It will keep working for as long as browsers do.

Every tool states its statistical assumptions and what it does not cover. Where a better method exists — GeoLift for geo tests, always-valid inference for continuous monitoring — the page says so and links to it, rather than letting you believe this was the last word.
